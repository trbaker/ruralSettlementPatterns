Where do farmers put their houses?

A rural settlement is a place where most people make a living from the land — farming, herding, fishing, forestry. Its population is small and spread thin, and the houses are arranged in one of three recognizable ways. This page uses live ArcGIS imagery so you can see each pattern on the real landscape, then practice spotting it.

Clustered (nucleated)DispersedLinear
What it looks like Houses, barns, church, and shops grouped tightly in a village; farmland surrounds the village. Each farm family lives on its own land, isolated from neighbors; houses spread evenly across the landscape. Houses form a line along a road, river, canal, or coastline; fields stretch back from the line.
Why it forms Defense, shared wells and pastures, social and religious life, communal or feudal land tenure (open-field system), scarce farmland. Abundant land, private ownership, large mechanized farms, the enclosure movement, government surveys that handed out rectangular plots. Everyone wants frontage on the transport route or water source; long-lot surveys give each farm a narrow strip touching it.
Where you see it Medieval European villages, New England colonial towns, much of rural Asia and Africa, Spanish colonial plaza towns. U.S. Midwest and Great Plains, rural Canada, Australia, Britain after enclosure. Québec along the St. Lawrence, Louisiana bayous, the Texas Rio Grande, German Strassendorf road villages.
Survey system link Open-field strips around the village; metes and bounds in irregular terrain. Township and range (Public Land Survey System): square sections, one farmstead per plot. French long-lot: narrow, deep parcels perpendicular to a river or road.
Sub-forms Circular (German Rundling, southern African kraal), grid (New England, colonial Spanish). — River-front, road-front, coastal.

Check yourself

Multiple choice first, then four free-response prompts written the way the exam asks them.

Free-response practice

Write your answer first, then open the sample. On the exam, "describe" means give a full sentence with a specific detail, not just a label.

Prompt A · Clustered

Describe ONE reason clustered rural settlements were common in medieval Europe.

Sample: Under the feudal open-field system, peasants farmed strips scattered across large shared fields and needed to live close together to share plows, draft animals, and the village common. Living in a nucleated village also made it easier to defend against raiders and to reach the church and market.

Prompt B · Dispersed

Explain how the township-and-range survey system contributed to a dispersed settlement pattern in the United States.

Sample: The Public Land Survey System divided the Midwest into one-square-mile sections and sold or granted them as 160-acre quarter sections to individual families. Because each family received its own large, rectangular plot, farmers built their houses on their own land rather than in a village, producing isolated farmsteads spread evenly across the landscape.

Prompt C · Linear

Identify the survey system associated with the linear settlement pattern along the St. Lawrence River and describe how it shaped the landscape.

Sample: The French long-lot system. Each farm was a narrow strip running back from the river so that every family had access to the water for transportation, fishing, and irrigation. Houses were built at the river end of each lot, so from the air the settlement appears as a line of houses along the bank with long fields stretching behind them.

Prompt D · Survey systems

Compare the metes-and-bounds and township-and-range survey systems, and explain how each influenced the rural settlement pattern of the region where it was used.

Sample: Metes and bounds describes property using natural landmarks such as trees, streams, and boulders along with distances and compass directions, producing irregular parcels of many sizes; it was used in the original thirteen colonies, where settlement followed the terrain and was often clustered in villages or scattered unevenly along creeks. Township and range, created by the Land Ordinance of 1785, imposed a grid of six-mile-square townships divided into one-square-mile sections regardless of terrain; because families received their own square quarter sections, they built isolated farmsteads, giving the Midwest and Great Plains a dispersed pattern with roads running along the section lines.
